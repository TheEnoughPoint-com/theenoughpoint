**Category A COE closed at S$139,001 in the first November bidding, the highest Category A price in records back to 2002. If your car can last another year or two, waiting for a cheaper certificate sounds sensible.**

The question is what the wait is meant to deliver. A cheaper COE needs more certificates, weaker demand or a change in the rules. We checked all three. The evidence is strongest on supply, and supply has a timetable.

The short answer: unusually large groups of cars reach the end of their first COE between now and September 2029. That supports supply for roughly the next three years. After that, much smaller groups follow. Waiting past that point gives up the supply support. A recession could still bring prices down, but its timing is unknown.

## Will COE prices drop in 2027?

Nobody can forecast COE prices, and we do not try. What the data can show is supply, and **supply in 2027 does not point to a large drop on its own.**

Under our reference case, where 45% of owners renew, Category A and B supply in 2027 runs at about 4,400 to 4,600 certificates a month. That is close to the 4,280 a month on offer now. If renewals stay near 2025's level of about 31%, supply climbs to about 5,300 a month by January 2028, roughly a quarter more than now.

So the supply case for a lower price in 2027 rests on how many owners let their cars go. Demand and rules matter as much. LTA expects to report on the proposed merger of Categories A and B in the first half of 2027. That would shift costs between models, not add certificates.

The larger supply change comes after 2029, when smaller groups of cars reach ten. [The supply paths are charted below](#how-many-certificates-could-come-back), with every quarter's numbers.

## Where new COEs come from

A COE, or Certificate of Entitlement, is the right to own a car for ten years. You buy one at auction, in a monthly bidding exercise. Cars are split into Category A, for smaller and less powerful models, and Category B, for the rest.

Singapore has no growth in its car numbers until at least January 2028. So almost every new certificate replaces a car that has left the roads. The biggest single source is cars whose first COE ends, at about age ten.

That makes the age of today's cars a guide to future supply. LTA's September age table shows how many cars will reach ten in each of the next five years.

<CoeCohorts />

The next three groups average **79,149 cars a year**. The two after that average **47,662**, about 40% fewer.

Two cautions. Not every car reaching ten becomes a new certificate, because many owners renew. And the age table combines all categories, so it cannot show separate peaks for Category A and B.

In plain English: **there is a three-year stretch of unusually large replacement supply, and then a smaller one.**

## More certificates have usually meant cheaper COEs

This is the pattern that makes the age table matter. The chart below puts twenty-four years of COE prices above the number of certificates offered.

<CoeHistory />

Supply fell from 2007 to 2013 while prices climbed. When supply recovered in 2016 to 2019, prices fell back.

Was the 2008 crash doing all the work? It was not. **Over any twelve months in which supply rose, COE prices tended to fall. When supply fell, prices tended to rise.** The pattern holds for both categories, with or without the 2008 to 2010 crisis, and in the years since 2012.

The link is loose, though. In 2025, supply rose and prices rose too. Loan limits, EV incentives and buyers rushing before rule changes all move prices as well. Supply is one force among several.

## How many certificates could come back?

Here is where renewals decide the answer. An owner who renews keeps the car, and that car's deregistration does not feed new supply.

We do not know how many owners will renew. LTA does not publish a renewal rate for cars reaching ten. We estimated one from the age tables: the share of nine-year-old cars still registered a year later, after passing ten. Between 2016 and 2025 it ranged from about 27% to 60%. The yearly median was 44.6%. In 2025 it was 30.8%.

So we ran three paths, with 30%, 45% and 60% of owners renewing. Everything else stays the same in all three.

<CoeScenarios />

Under the 45% reference, supply when the largest group feeds through is about **4,700 a month**. That is only a little above the 4,280 a month LTA has announced for November 2026 to January 2027. With fewer renewals, at 30%, it is about **5,700**. With more, at 60%, it is about 3,800.

Why so much lower than 2016, when supply reached about 7,400 a month? Three things differ. The group reaching ten in 2016 was bigger, about 108,000 cars against 84,000 in the largest group ahead. Only about 27% of those owners renewed, so roughly 79,000 cars left the roads. At 45% renewal, about 46,000 of the coming group would leave. And LTA now deducts about 6,300 certificates a year, because it released them early from 2023.

What would it take to get back to about 7,000 a month? Three things at once: renewals as low as 2016's 27%, as many other cars leaving as in 2019, about 32,000, and no deduction for early releases. Each has happened in some year. They have not happened together. **That combination is the top of the pale band.** It is not a ceiling, and it is not a probability range. Going higher would also need LTA to release extra certificates.

Renewals cut both ways, though. A renewal holds a car out of the supply pipeline for five or ten more years. The Ministry of Transport has said that more renewals when prices are high would reduce the supply of new COEs. But a renewal can also remove a bidder: an owner who would otherwise scrap the car and buy a new one is not in the auction. **So renewals cut supply and can also cut demand.** Our supply paths do not estimate which effect is larger for prices.

In plain English: **the large age groups mostly keep supply near today's level for three years, unless far fewer owners renew. The clearer change is the drop after 2029.**

## Do job losses bring COE prices down?

Many buyers wait for a downturn, on the view that fewer people bid when jobs are scarce. We tested that against the Ministry of Manpower's unemployment rate since 2002, and fixed the tests before running them.

<CoeJobs />

The 2009 recession stands out. Unemployment rose to 3.3%, and Category A averaged S$3,863 in the first quarter of 2009. Over the whole period, years when unemployment rose tended to be years when COE prices fell. Take out 2008 to 2010 and that pattern mostly fades. Once the number of certificates is allowed for, what is left could be chance.

The timing does not help a waiting buyer either. **A rise in unemployment did not come before falls in COE prices.** In 2009, prices recovered while unemployment was still at its high. In 2020, unemployment rose to 3.4% and COE prices rose, as fewer certificates came out.

We ran similar tests on share prices, the STI and the S&P 500. Rallies were not a dependable signal either.

That does not mean jobs never matter. A deep shock to incomes and credit can cut demand, as 2009 showed. It means **rising unemployment has not been a dependable signal to wait for.**

## Renew for five years, ten, or replace?

If your car is near ten, the price that matters most may be the renewal price, not the auction. Renewing does not involve bidding. The owner pays the Prevailing Quota Premium, or PQP: the average COE price of the previous three months with bidding. For November 2026 it is S$129,883 for Category A and S$131,819 for Category B.

Which month's PQP you pay depends on timing. Renew in the month the COE expires, or up to a month late with a fee, and you pay that month's PQP. Renew earlier and you pay the PQP of the month before expiry, and give up the unused part of the old COE.

<CoeRenewalPaths />

Three things drive the comparison.

- **Both renewals cost about 10% of the PQP for each year kept.** Five years at half the PQP, or ten years at the full PQP.
- **Renewing gives up the PARF rebate.** A car reaching ten now is on a COE from before February 2023. Deregistered at nine to ten years, it returns 50% of the ARF it paid. A renewed car gets no PARF rebate at the end.
- **The ten-year renewal needs twice the cash, but it can be repeated.** If the car leaves before the ten years are up, the unused part of the PQP comes back as a COE rebate.

The calculator also counts the replacement's smaller rebate at the end. Cars on COEs from February 2026 get 5% of their ARF at nine to ten years, capped at S$30,000, against 50% for today's ten-year-old cars. **For an owner, the renewal price and the rebate rules can matter more than where the auction goes next.**

## Rules that move the bill

Three policy changes matter for the total cost, not only the COE.

- **EV tax relief steps down.** An electric car can get up to S$30,000 off its Additional Registration Fee (ARF) if registered in 2026, and up to S$20,000 in 2027. For a buyer who qualifies for the maximum, a COE would need to fall about S$10,000 just to offset that change.
- **A smaller rebate at the end.** Cars on COEs from February 2026 get a lower PARF rebate when deregistered: 30% of ARF in the first five years, falling to 5% in the tenth year, capped at S$30,000. That lowers what the car is worth when you let it go.
- **A possible merger of Categories A and B.** LTA has consulted on one passenger-car category, with a rebate or surcharge of up to S$15,000 by model. It is a proposal. LTA expects to share findings in the first half of 2027. It would not add certificates; it would shift costs between models. Whether it would touch renewals is undecided.

Private-hire cars owned by individuals have also faced the same loan limits as private cars since 30 October 2026. That may soften some demand. One bidding exercise since is too few to show any effect.

## So, buy now or wait?

For someone already planning to buy, the supply case is strongest over roughly the next three years. Quotas stay supported by large groups of expiring cars into late 2029. After that, smaller groups follow, and supply in every path we ran falls through 2030.

**That is a window, not a deadline.** LTA's quota uses a rolling year of deregistrations, so the change reaches auctions gradually. LTA can also bring supply forward, as it did with five-year renewals from May 2023. Those cars' later exits are now deducted from current quotas, because their certificates were already issued. Further redistribution could make a later peak smaller and a later trough shallower.

**It is also not a promise that earlier is cheaper.** Under the 45% reference, supply over the window is close to today's. Prices will depend on demand as much as on certificates. Waiting indefinitely for a lower COE, however, means waiting into years with less supply support.

Waiting for a recession is a different decision. A deep downturn could lower prices, as in 2008. Nobody knows when one will come. It may also hit your income or your access to a car loan. That is a bet on timing, and the supply evidence does not require it. The usual warning signs are a poor clock as well, as [our test of rising interest rates since 1954](/rates-rising-market-about-to-crash/) showed. Share investors face the same trade-off: [the dip people wait for turns up about a third of the time](/waiting-for-a-dip/).

The cheap, boring checks:

**Set the all-in price you would accept.** That is the COE, the car, the ARF after any EV relief, and the lower PARF value at the end. If you already own a car near ten, [set it against the renewal price](#renew-for-five-years-ten-or-replace), which LTA publishes shortly before each month begins. If you wait, the car fund still needs a safe home: here is [how T-bills, SSBs and cash accounts differ](/cash-management-platforms-compared/).

**Watch the quarterly quota.** LTA announces each quarter's quota at the end of January, April, July and October. If the Category A and B numbers keep holding up or rising, the supply window is doing its work. The supply calculator under the scenario chart shows what different renewal rates would mean.

**Decide what you would do if prices do not fall.** If you would still buy, the question is only when within the window. If you would not, the decision is about the car, not the COE.

<details class="rc-fold">
<summary>Where our numbers are soft</summary>
<div class="rc-fold-body">

- **The renewal share is a proxy.** It compares cars aged ten with the same group a year earlier. Early exits, imported used cars and late renewals all blur it. It is not a measured probability of renewal.
- **Other exits are a residual.** We take total Category A and B deregistrations and subtract the estimated exits of cars reaching ten. What is left includes older cars, early exits and classification differences. We hold it at the 2023 to 2025 average of about 23,000 a year.
- **The scenarios are mechanical.** Each quota quarter uses the previous twelve months of modelled deregistrations, less the current yearly deduction for certificates released early (about 6,300), with about 10% going to the open category. Fed with actual deregistrations, the same formula reproduces LTA's announced quota for November 2026 to January 2027. Future deductions, injections and any change to the five-year renewal are not known and are not included.
- **The band is not a confidence interval.** It crosses the lowest and highest historical renewal and other-exit values, with and without that deduction. These combinations never occurred together. Actual supply can fall outside it.
- **The 2016 comparison mixes two tables.** The 2016 group is cars aged nine at the end of 2015 in LTA's annual age table. The coming groups come from its monthly table at 30 September 2026. Both count cars, but their coverage differs slightly. The 27% is the same renewal proxy as above.
- **Age is an approximate expiry date.** Cars bought used, renewed early or registered late shift their timing. The age table cannot separate Category A from B.
- **The supply and price link is an association.** Using twelve-month changes, the rank correlation between supply and premiums is about −0.5 for Category A and −0.55 for Category B over April 2002 to October 2026, and similar outside July 2008 to June 2010 and after 2012. It does not measure how much a given change in supply moves prices.
- **The jobs tests.** Quarterly, April 2002 to September 2026, without April to June 2020. We regressed the one-year change in each category's premium on the one-year change in the unemployment rate, with the change in quota as a control. Separately, we tested whether changes in unemployment over the previous one, two or four quarters came before premium changes. We fixed the tests before running them. No full-sample result held up after adjusting for the number of tests. At the main four-quarter horizon, the earlier-unemployment results pointed the other way. Retrenchment numbers gave the same direction as unemployment. Our earlier tests of STI and S&P 500 returns were also inconclusive.
- **The renewal calculator is simple arithmetic.** It spreads each cost evenly over the years kept and ignores interest, which favours options that need less cash now. It assumes the current car is between nine and ten years old on a COE from before February 2023.
