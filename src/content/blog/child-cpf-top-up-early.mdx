Putting money into a small child’s CPF Special Account looks like the easy part of parenting maths. The Special Account is CPF’s retirement-savings account for a member below 55; since January 2025 it closes at 55. For the quarter from 1 July to 30 September 2026 it earns 4% a year, its floor rate, with an extra 1% on the first S$60,000 of combined CPF balances for a member below 55. A dollar placed there when a child is one has more time to compound than the same dollar placed there at 21.

That arithmetic is real. It is also not the whole decision.

The Ministry of Manpower’s written answer to Parliament on 2 April 2024 gives the question some scale. As of 31 December 2023, 3,400 children under the age of five had balances in their Special Account, and the median sum was around S$1,000. Those are the Ministry’s figures, not ours. The same answer says CPF Board does not keep track of whether the top-ups came from parents.

The two figures are useful. If the compounding case were the whole story, a large early top-up would be ordinary. It is not. The missing part is what the top-up converts: money a parent can still use becomes retirement savings that cannot be withdrawn for any other purpose once accepted.

## The rate is not 5% on the whole balance

CPF Board’s extra-interest rule is easy to misread. It is an extra 1% on the first S$60,000 of a member’s **combined** CPF balances, not a flat 5% on every dollar in the Special Account. For a child with no other CPF savings, the first S$60,000 of Special Account savings earns 5% under the current rule; the balance above that earns the 4% Special Account rate. In practice a Singapore-citizen child already holds a MediSave balance from the MediSave Grant for Newborns, S$4,000 for births from 1 January 2015 to 31 March 2025 and S$5,000 from 1 April 2025, and that balance uses part of the S$60,000 first.

The difference is not a technical footnote. Take an established S$100,000 Special Account balance, no other CPF savings and no further deposits or withdrawals. The table applies the July-to-September 2026 rates repeatedly solely to display the tiering. It is an arithmetic illustration, not a forecast of CPF rates or a prediction of a child’s balance. CPF computes interest monthly and credits it annually; this annual schedule gives the same result where the balance and rates are held constant through each year.

**In plain English:** early compounding adds years, but it does not turn every Special Account dollar into a perpetual 5% dollar. A large balance needs two rates in the calculation.

That is not an argument against the rate. It is a reason to describe the rate precisely before using it to settle a decades-long question.

## What an early top-up buys — and what it spends

The Retirement Sum Topping-Up Scheme is the route for a cash top-up to a child’s Special Account. Every top-up does two things at once: it earns interest under the current CPF rules, and it changes what that dollar can be used for. The three points below explain the trade. None settles the question alone.

### 1. Once the money goes in, it is out of reach until the child turns 55, and then only as retirement savings

CPF Board says top-ups are irreversible and cannot be withdrawn for any other purpose. When the child turns 55, the top-ups and the interest they have earned move into the Retirement Account, up to the Full Retirement Sum, and the Special Account closes; that money is reserved for monthly payouts, which under today’s rules can start at any time between 65 and 70. For a child born in 2025, that is 2080 at the earliest, with payouts from 2090 at the earliest. The money can no longer be used for school fees, a move, a medical gap or a change in plans. The interest rate is what CPF pays on money accepted for retirement; it does not make the loss of access disappear. Whether the family may need that access is a separate question from the rate.

### 2. A top-up to a child does not reduce the parent’s tax bill

CPF Board says expressly that no tax relief is given for top-ups to a child’s CPF accounts. The parent can still separately make a top-up to their own account, with relief if their own Special Account is below the Full Retirement Sum; [how that compares with an SRS contribution](/cpf-vs-srs-top-up/) is a separate question. But this payment does not produce relief for the person who makes it. “CPF top-up” sounds like one tax category, but the tax result depends on whose account receives the money.

### 3. It may leave the adult child with less room for a CPF top-up later

For a CPF member below 55, the maximum cash top-up is the current Full Retirement Sum less Special Account savings and amounts withdrawn from the Special Account for investment. The Full Retirement Sum is CPF’s published retirement-savings reference amount, S$220,400 for 2026. The more already in the Special Account, the less room remains under that formula.

That room has a later use. IRAS allows CPF Cash Top-up Relief of up to S$8,000 a year for cash top-ups to one’s own account, provided the account is below the Full Retirement Sum. An adult child who works and pays tax may want that capacity, and money already in the Special Account reduces what can be added at that point. The word *may* matters: relief needs income and a tax position, the account may stay far enough below the cap for the distinction never to bind, and no tax saving follows automatically from spare capacity. The cost is a future option that may or may not become valuable, not a tax bill charged today.

**In plain English:** money put in now takes up room the adult child might have wanted for a tax-relieved top-up of their own, but only if they come close to the cap in a year when the relief would matter.

## The missing third path: invest outside CPF

The alternative is not always cash. A parent may keep the money outside CPF and take equity risk over a long period. That can offer a higher potential return, but it replaces CPF’s current crediting rule with a market value that can fall, sometimes materially. A long horizon gives more time to ride through price fluctuations; it does not make an 8% annual outcome certain.

This is where a rate comparison becomes misleading. “CPF at 5% versus equities at 8%” puts a current CPF tier beside an assumed, volatile equity outcome. The first is governed by CPF’s current rules and applies only to the relevant tier; the second needs a chosen market, currency, fee level, tax treatment and holding period. The two numbers can be useful as labelled scenarios, but neither should be presented as the return a child will receive.

There is a further distinction. An equity investment held in a parent’s account is not the same gift as a CPF top-up to the child: the control and ownership arrangements differ. This article does not prescribe an account structure. It treats that difference as a decision that must be made explicit, rather than buried inside a return assumption.

There is also a nearer comparison than equities. The same dollar in the parent’s own Special Account earns the same 4% floor, carries CPF Cash Top-up Relief of up to S$8,000 a year if the parent’s own Special Account is below the Full Retirement Sum, and pays out to the parent from the parent’s 65 rather than to the child from the child’s. It is locked just the same, for decades fewer. What the child’s account offers that the parent’s cannot, if the parent’s combined balances are already past S$60,000, is the extra 1%. The second table prices that difference on S$10,000, again with today’s tiers held constant.

|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 10 years | S$16,289 | S$14,802 | S$1,487 |

Two ceilings do different jobs here: the Full Retirement Sum formula caps what can enter through the top-up route, the S$8,000 figure caps the relief on a person’s own top-ups, neither is a target, and unused Special Account room is not cash.

## A rising cap opens space, but does not reverse the trade

There is a real counterweight. The Full Retirement Sum rises over time: CPF Board’s published schedule moves from S$181,000 for the 2020 cohort to S$220,400 for 2026 and S$228,200 for 2027, a step of S$7,800. That opens absolute room for more top-ups. It does not restore the difference created by an earlier top-up: two otherwise identical accounts receive the same S$7,800 rise, and the one with the earlier S$1,000 top-up stays lower in available room by that top-up and the interest credited on it.

A rising cap reopens capacity; it does not undo the opportunity cost of leaving the money outside CPF. The narrower question is whether the child’s likely Special Account balance will sit close enough to the prevailing cap, in a year when they have taxable income, for the difference to matter. The public rules cannot answer that for any household.

## What changes with the child’s age

The question is not answered by one age.

**An earlier top-up** gives the money more time under the rate and extra-interest rules that apply along the way. It also makes the irreversible decision earlier, when the family has less information about what the child will need before retirement. It creates the maximum interval in which the balance can occupy capacity that the adult child might otherwise have used.

**A later top-up** has fewer years of compounding. It also leaves cash under family control for longer and postpones the point at which the child’s Special Account balance begins to reduce their own top-up room. Neither sentence is a recommendation. They are the two sides of the same date.

The Ministry’s median of around S$1,000 is useful here as a description of reported balances, not as a target. It shows that the choice is not only about imagined six-figure balances. Even a modest payment asks the same question: is this dollar for retirement, or is it a dollar whose job has not yet been decided?

The answer can also change without anyone changing their mind about CPF. A family’s liquid savings, housing plans, education commitments and the child’s eventual earning path are not fixed when the child is small. The rule remains the same while the surrounding facts change. That is why a published rate cannot supply a single age at which the trade becomes correct for every family.

## Four checks before the rate does the talking

No product comparison is needed to make the trade visible.

**First: what is this dollar for?** A dollar intended for the child’s retirement has a different job from a dollar that might be needed before the child starts work. Writing down that distinction does not decide the top-up; it makes the lock-up visible.

**Second: has the Child Development Account reached its co-matching cap?** The Government matches savings into a child’s CDA dollar for dollar up to a cap, and CDA money can be spent on approved uses such as childcare, kindergarten fees and healthcare before the child turns 12. As at 23 August 2026 the caps are S$4,000 for a first child, S$7,000 for a second, S$9,000 for a third or fourth and S$15,000 for a fifth or later. Under the SG Child Support Package announced at the 2026 National Day Rally, children born on or after 1 April 2027 receive the new terms: a uniform co-matching cap of S$5,000, with the account extended to the end of the year the child turns 16. For children born before that date, the existing caps are retained until 30 September 2027 and move to the uniform S$5,000 from 1 October 2027. For a dollar that would still be matched, the match is worth more than any year of interest, and the money stays usable for the child.

**Third: what happens outside CPF?** “Outside CPF” can mean cash that remains available ([where it earns a published rate](/cash-management-platforms-compared/)), an equity investment whose future sale value varies, or the same dollar in the parent’s own Special Account. The question is not merely which number compounds faster. It is whether the money can remain invested through a fall ([waiting for one before investing is its own trap](/waiting-for-a-dip/)), and who controls it while the child is young.

**Fourth: how much CPF top-up room is left today?** Check the current Special Account balance and the current Full Retirement Sum. The cap is a moving gap, not a permanent figure copied from an old article. CPF publishes the applicable amount, and the account record shows the other side of the subtraction. The parent’s payment to a child does not receive CPF Cash Top-up Relief; the adult child’s possible future relief is a separate question.

## The real question: accessible money, retirement money or long-term risk capital?

The 5% applies to the first S$60,000 of combined CPF balances under the current rules; the rest earns 4%. That is a reason the question deserves care, not a reason it answers itself. **A child’s Special Account top-up is an irrevocable retirement gift.** It is not a child-savings account with a better headline rate.

The key question is therefore not “is 5% attractive?” or “can equities make 8%?” It is whether this particular dollar is accessible family money, retirement money already, or capital that can genuinely stay invested through an uncertain market path. Each answer gives the dollar a different job. The last two both carry uncertainty: the market’s is in the price; CPF’s is in the rules, which every illustration here holds constant and the law does not. The 4% floor is extended a year at a time, currently to 31 December 2026, and the Special Account itself was closed for members aged 55 and above on 19 January 2025.

That is more decisive than “act sooner” or “wait”. It divides a rate question from a classification question.
