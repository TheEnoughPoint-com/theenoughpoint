**Yes, if two things are true: the fund is registered in Ireland, and you are not a US citizen and do not live permanently in the US. Buying on SGX does not protect you by itself. The S&P 500 fund already on SGX, SPDR S&P 500 (code S27), is registered in the US, so the US can tax it when its owner dies.**

What changes on 13 October is access. From that Tuesday, four index funds registered in Ireland, one tracking the S&P 500, trade on SGX in Singapore dollars, and SRS money can buy them. Compared with S27, the Irish S&P 500 fund carries no US estate tax, which on S$100,000 of S27 would be about S$6,071, and costs an estimated 0.19% a year less. If you already own an Irish fund overseas, the gain is small, and switching can cost more than it saves.

## What changes, and what does not

A fund is a company or a trust, and its domicile is the country where it is registered: its legal home. If you are not a US citizen and do not live permanently in the US, the US can tax the American assets you own at death. The tax starts above **US$60,000** (S$76,650 at the 18 September 2026 exchange rate) and rises to 40%. The tax calls these US-situated assets, and they include US shares and US-registered funds. [The estate piece](/us-estate-tax-singapore-investors/) has the mechanics. Shares in an Irish-registered fund do not count, even when the fund holds the same American companies.

Irish funds are not new. They have traded for years on overseas exchanges such as London, including the same Xtrackers S&P 500 share class at the same 0.03% fee. Buying them needed access to that overseas market, usually in US dollars. Whether that meant a second broker depended on the account you already had.

The new part is SRS, the Supplementary Retirement Scheme: a voluntary retirement account whose contributions reduce your taxable income. SGX states that SRS money can buy any ETF listed on SGX, through a broker linked to the SRS account. S27 is second in SGX's table of the ten ETFs held by SRS investors, as of October 2025. From 13 October, the same exchange also lists Irish-domiciled funds. CPF money is a different case: none of the four is on CPF Board's list of included ETFs, last updated 17 September 2025.

## Three S&P 500 funds on one basis

The comparison adds two costs: the fund's annual fee, and the part of US dividends lost to US tax. A US fund's ordinary dividend distributions to a Singapore holder generally lose 30%, and S27 pays its dividends out after deducting its fees. An Irish fund loses 15% of its US dividends under the US–Ireland tax treaty, inside the fund. With the S&P 500 yielding 1.04% (22 September 2026), the estimates are:

| Fund (registered in; where it trades) | Fee + dividend tax a year | On S$100,000 a year | US estate tax on S$100,000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| S27 (US; SGX, in US$) | 0.38% | S$378 | about S$6,071 |
| CSPX (Ireland; London, in US$) | 0.23% | S$226 | none |
| Xtrackers S&P 500 4C (Ireland; SGX, in S$) | 0.19% | S$186 | none |

<p class="sx-quiet">Estimates under stated assumptions, not measured performance. They leave out tracking difference, securities-lending income, and platform, custody and dealing costs. The estate column assumes a holder who is neither a US citizen nor US-domiciled, with this as their only US-situated asset, at S$1.2775 per US dollar (18 September 2026).</p>

Against S27, the fee-and-tax difference is about 0.19% a year (S$192 on S$100,000), before dealing costs. A lower fee does not guarantee an equal difference in returns; how closely each fund tracks the index decides that.

The larger difference is the estate tax. S27 held at death is taxed on everything above US$60,000, at rates that climb to 40%, so the bill grows faster than the holding. The Irish funds are not taxed at all:

| Held at death | US estate tax: S27 | US estate tax: Irish fund |
|---|---|---|
| S$100,000 | S$6,071 (6% of the holding) | none |
| S$300,000 | S$67,639 (23%) | none |
| S$500,000 | S$135,252 (27%) | none |

<p class="sx-quiet">For a holder who is neither a US citizen nor US-domiciled, with this as their only US-situated asset, at S$1.2775 per US dollar (18 September 2026). Other US shares or US funds add to the same total.</p>

The fee difference is paid every year you hold. The estate tax is paid once, by your family, and only if you die holding the US fund.

## If you already hold an Irish fund overseas

You already have the domicile treatment. The SGX line does not improve it.

The fee saving depends on what you hold. Against CSPX it is 0.04% a year: a 0.07% fee against 0.03%, or S$40 on S$100,000. Against the same Xtrackers share class in London, it is nothing: same fund, same fee.

A switch is a sale and a purchase, each with a commission and a spread, and possibly a currency conversion. If those came to 0.2% of the holding, a saving of 0.04% a year would take about five years to recover; at 0.4%, about ten. That assumes both funds track the index equally well. Their reported tracking differences show whether they do.

## What the listing does not change

<div class="sx-beat"><b>Currency.</b> The SGX lines are quoted in Singapore dollars but hold US-dollar assets, and they are not hedged. If the US dollar falls against the Singapore dollar, the holding falls with it, as S27 and CSPX do.</div>

<div class="sx-beat"><b>Dealing costs, until trading starts.</b> The spread is the gap between the price to buy and the price to sell. Nobody can see it before 13 October. As an illustration only: a 0.40% spread means paying about 0.20% above the midpoint to buy. If that were 0.20% more than buying the alternative, the fee-and-tax gap would recover it in about a year against S27, and about five years against CSPX.</div>

<div class="sx-beat"><b>Dividend tax is reduced, not removed.</b> The 15% is paid inside the fund, so you never see a bill. It applies to US dividends; the MSCI World fund's other dividends face other countries' rates.</div>

## Tax on death: what the fund's legal home settles

Two conditions matter. If the holder is neither a US citizen nor US-domiciled, US estate tax reaches only US-situated assets, and the Irish unit is not one. If neither the holder nor the heir is domiciled or ordinarily resident in Ireland, each tested at their own relevant date, Ireland's inheritance tax exempts units in Irish funds. Singapore removed estate duty for deaths from 15 February 2008. Under those conditions, no estate or inheritance tax falls on the unit itself.

**SRS is different.** When an SRS account holder dies, the whole balance is treated as withdrawn on that date. Up to S$400,000 is exempt, less if withdrawals had begun, and half of the rest is taxed as the holder's income. That is income tax, not estate duty, and it applies whatever the account holds.

The paperwork is also separate from the tax. The IRS transfer certificate, a clearance that US assets can pass, asks for a list of every US asset the person held, whatever its tax status. An Irish fund takes the holding out of the US tax; it does not settle every document a custodian may ask the family for.

In plain English: the fund's legal home settles the estate-tax question. It does not settle the SRS income tax or the paperwork.

## A check for this week

1. **Find each fund's legal home.** It is on the fund's factsheet. A fund's ISIN, its international security code, usually starts with that country: US for S27, IE for the Irish funds.
2. **Add up your US-situated holdings across every account, cash and SRS,** against the US$60,000 threshold. The threshold counts all of them together, including shares of US companies held directly.
3. **Price any change before making it.** Commissions, spreads and currency conversion, set against the fee difference.
4. **Tell someone where the accounts are.** A list of accounts is the cheapest part of an estate plan.

The rule has not changed. What changed on 13 October is which accounts can reach a fund on the other side of it.

<summary><b>Method and assumptions</b>: what the estimates include and leave out.</summary>

- **Fee plus dividend tax.** Irish funds: fee + 15% × index dividend yield. S27: fee + 30% × (index dividend yield − fee), because S27 distributes dividends net of its fees and expenses; if the yield is at or below the fee, the dividend tax is taken as zero. At 1.04%: S27 0.0945% + 0.284% = 0.378%; CSPX 0.07% + 0.156% = 0.226%; Xtrackers 0.03% + 0.156% = 0.186%.
- **The 30% rate** is the general US rate on ordinary dividends a US fund pays a holder with no tax treaty. IRS Publication 515 and the fund's prospectus describe exceptions, such as distributions paid out of US interest or short-term capital gains; the estimate ignores them.
- **The 15% rate** applies to US-source dividends received by an Irish fund. It says nothing about the non-US dividends of the MSCI World fund.
- **Left out:** tracking difference, securities-lending income, platform and custody fees, and dealing costs (the calculator adds the last as inputs).
- **Estate figures** apply the published US rate schedule less the US$13,000 credit, to a holder who is neither a US citizen nor US-domiciled, with no other US-situated assets. Holding in S$ is converted to US$, taxed in US$ and converted back. Body examples use S$1.2775 per US dollar (18 September 2026); the calculator uses the rate refreshed at each site build.
- **Spreads.** Buying at the offer costs half the quoted spread against the midpoint between buy and sell prices, not necessarily against the fund's net asset value. Recovery years = extra one-off cost ÷ annual saving, both as a share of the holding: 0.20% ÷ 0.192% a year ≈ 1.0 year against S27; 0.20% ÷ 0.04% a year = 5 years against CSPX.
- **Transfer certificate.** The IRS says it takes 12 to 18 months to process a request made without a Form 706-NA return, counted from when it has every document. That is a processing time, not a timetable for settling an estate or releasing assets.
