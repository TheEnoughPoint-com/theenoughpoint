**The US estate-tax rule has not changed. What has changed is where the fix is sold: from 13 October, on SGX, in Singapore dollars, on the exchange SRS money already uses.**

If you own US shares or a US-domiciled fund, the US taxes that holding when you die once it passes **US$60,000**, about S$76,650 today, at rates up to 40%. [The estate piece](/us-estate-tax-singapore-investors) has the mechanics. The standard way around it is a fund domiciled in Ireland. It holds the same companies, but what you own is a share of an Irish company, and the US does not count that.

The catch was the doorway. Those Irish funds traded in London, in US dollars. You needed a broker with London access and a currency conversion. In practice you also needed cash, because SRS money reaches SGX-listed securities and locally offered funds, not London listings. On SGX itself, the S&P 500 fund was S27, which is a US trust: inside the estate rule, and losing 30% of its dividends to US tax.

From **Tuesday 13 October 2026**, four Irish-domiciled index funds run by DWS, Deutsche Bank's asset manager, trade on SGX in Singapore dollars under its Xtrackers brand. MAS registered their Singapore prospectus on 14 September.

None of them is a new fund. Each SGX line is an existing share class, with the same ISIN that already trades in London and Frankfurt. The fund's size, record and costs are already public.

## What actually changed

<div class="sx-door"><b>SRS money.</b> Until now, SRS money buying the S&P 500 on SGX bought a US trust. The other road was an index unit trust through a fund platform, with its own running costs on top. An Irish S&P 500 line now trades where [SRS money](/cpf-vs-srs-top-up/) already goes. MOF lists ETFs as a permitted SRS investment, but your SRS operator and broker enable each counter. Confirm these codes with yours before you place an order.</div>

<div class="sx-door"><b>No second broker, no second currency.</b> Before, the Irish route meant London hours, a US-dollar conversion and a broker with UK access. Now it trades in Singapore hours, in Singapore dollars, on the exchange you already use.</div>

<div class="sx-door"><b>The SGX default has another option.</b> The S&P 500 fund most Singapore investors bought on this exchange sits inside the estate rule. The same exchange now lists an S&P 500 fund outside it.</div>

In plain English: the fix used to need a second broker, a second currency and cash. Now it sits on SGX, in the same account you already use.

## What it costs, like for like

A fund's price tag understates what it costs to own. The US also keeps part of the dividends, and how much depends on where the fund is domiciled. A US fund paying a Singapore holder loses **30%**, because Singapore has no US tax treaty. An Irish fund loses **15%** under the US–Ireland treaty. With the S&P 500 yielding about 1.04%, the full running cost a year is:

- **S27, US trust on SGX:** 0.0945% charge + 0.31% dividend tax = **0.41%**
- **CSPX, Irish fund in London:** 0.07% + 0.16% = **0.23%**
- **Xtrackers S&P 500, Irish fund on SGX:** 0.03% + 0.16% = **0.19%**

On S$100,000, that is about S$406 a year against S$186. The estate side is larger. Held until death as someone's only US-situated asset, S$300,000 of S27 leaves about **S$67,600** of US estate tax. The same amount in either Irish fund leaves none.

In plain English: against the US trust, the Irish line on SGX is cheaper every year and removes the estate bill. Against the London fund, the difference is small, and the real gain is convenience, plus access for SRS money.

## If you already hold the London fund

The London fund already does what the new line does on estate tax and dividend tax. The only saving is the charge: 0.07% against 0.03%, or **0.04 points a year**.

Switching costs money. You sell one line and buy another, paying commissions and two spreads. A switch that costs 0.2% in total takes about **five years** to repay; one costing 0.4% takes ten. The new line mostly helps with the next dollar you invest, not the last one.

## What it does not fix

<div class="sx-beat"><b>1. A Singapore-dollar price, US-dollar assets.</b> The prospectus says plainly that the share class is not currency-hedged. The fund owns US shares priced in US dollars. If the US dollar falls 10% against the Singapore dollar, your holding falls by about the same in Singapore dollars. The London fund and S27 carry exactly the same exposure.</div>

<div class="sx-beat"><b>2. The first weeks cost more to trade.</b> A new counter starts with few buyers and sellers. The market maker, Flow Traders, quotes a spread nobody can observe before 13 October. Against S27, even a 0.40% spread is repaid within a year by the running-cost gap. Against the London fund, it takes five years. A limit order caps what you pay; a market order in a thin counter does not.</div>

<div class="sx-beat"><b>3. Dividend tax is halved, not removed.</b> The 15% is still paid inside the fund. You never see a bill; it shows up as a slightly lower fund value.</div>

<div class="sx-beat"><b>4. CPF money cannot buy it.</b> None of the four is on CPF Board's list of included ETFs, last updated 17 September 2025. The list's criteria favour three years of SGX trading history, so inclusion soon is unlikely.</div>

<div class="sx-beat"><b>5. Single US shares have no Irish twin.</b> UCITS rules, the EU fund rules these funds are built under, forbid a fund that holds one company. Nvidia held directly stays US-situated, whatever else you own.</div>

## Then does Ireland tax my estate?

It is the obvious next question, and the answer is on the record. Ireland has its own inheritance tax. But units in Irish investment funds are exempt when neither the person who died nor the heir is domiciled or ordinarily resident in Ireland. Irish Revenue sets this out in its guidance on section 75 of the Capital Acquisitions Tax Consolidation Act 2003. Singapore abolished estate duty in 2008.

There is a second difference, in the paperwork. The [IRS transfer certificate](/us-estate-tax-singapore-investors/), which the IRS says takes 12 to 18 months, concerns property located in the US. An Irish fund unit is not located in the US.

In plain English: for a Singapore-based holder leaving these units to a Singapore-based family, none of the three countries involved taxes the unit on death.

## A cheap check, this week

1. **Find the ISIN of every fund you hold.** It is on the fund's factsheet and usually in your broker's app. One starting with **US** is US-situated. One starting with **IE** is not.
2. **Add up the US-situated side across every account, cash and SRS.** The threshold counts all of it together, against US$60,000.
3. **If an SRS account holds S27, an Irish S&P 500 line now trades on the same exchange.** Whether and when to change is your decision. Inside SRS, a switch is a sale and a purchase: commissions and spreads, and no Singapore tax.
4. **Tell someone where the accounts are.** It is still the cheapest estate plan there is.

The rule has not moved. The question it asks is what happens to this holding on the day you are no longer here to manage it. From 13 October, SGX offers a cheaper answer than it did before.

<summary><b>Soft spots</b>: what we could not confirm, stated plainly.</summary>

- **Three of the four SGX codes.** The prospectus names each counter, but only XND's code appears in a primary document. XUS, XWR and EUS are the codes the distributor publishes; they are to be confirmed against SGX at listing.
- **SRS for these specific counters.** No DWS, SGX or operator-bank statement covers them. The route is general (SGX-listed ETFs are routinely bought with SRS money); the counter-level answer is your operator's.
- **The opening spread.** An estimate of 0.15–0.40% at listing is in circulation and attributed to DWS; we could not find it in any DWS document. The piece therefore uses 0.40% only as a stress case.
- **The unit-trust road for SRS.** Singapore-domiciled S&P 500 index unit trusts feeding Irish funds exist and are sold for SRS money; their full running costs vary by platform and are not compared here.
- **Securities lending.** All four funds may lend their holdings. Two DWS documents disagree on how lending revenue is split (70/15/15 in the Irish supplement; 82/9/9 in the 2026 key information documents). The factsheets show the revenue as a very small share of assets.
