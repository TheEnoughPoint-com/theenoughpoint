**A new ETF may buy you more of what you already own. Four of these funds hold the same five American companies.**

**The published fund fee is only one part of the decision.** Local trading hours, SRS access, the actual buying and selling prices, and the fund structure matter too.

**The timetable.** Four Xtrackers funds began trading on Tuesday 13 October 2026: S&P 500, Nasdaq-100, MSCI World and S&P 500 Equal Weight. Four iFAST funds follow on Thursday 22 October: Nikkei 225, MSCI Korea, MSCI Taiwan, and AI & Big Data. Each iFAST fund holds one Xtrackers fund.

[The earlier piece](/sgx-irish-etfs-us-estate-tax/) covers the US estate-tax mechanics. SRS means the Supplementary Retirement Scheme, whose savings can buy these SGX-listed ETFs.

## What can you buy that was missing?

**The additions fill gaps in the choice of markets.** SGX gains a fund tracking shares across developed countries, plus separate Japan, Korea and Taiwan index funds. The new US funds trade in Singapore dollars. The China, regional and Singapore ranges do not grow this round.

The June 2026 SGX report counted 53 ETFs and leveraged products, including 15 focused on China and 14 on Singapore. Adding these eight gives 61 before any other listings or removals since June. This is an illustration, not a verified October total.

## Different funds can own the same shares

**Different fund names can lead back to the same companies.** Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet are the S&P 500 fund's five largest holdings. They also make up a large part of the Nasdaq-100, AI and developed-world funds.

<SgxOverlap />

The overlapping funds also own different companies. Their shared holdings explain why adding a fund may increase an existing exposure. Equal weight reduces the biggest companies' influence by giving each company roughly the same share of the fund.

A country fund can also depend heavily on one company: TSMC is 30.2% of the Taiwan fund, while Samsung Electronics is 32.0% of the Korea fund including its non-voting preference shares. Those percentages are from DWS holdings at 30 September 2026. A country label does not mean your money is spread evenly across its companies.

## Where does the money go?

**Fees taken inside the fund.** The Xtrackers S&P 500 fund charges 0.03% a year whether bought in London or on SGX. iFAST ETFs have another layer: 0.25% a year plus running costs to hold a Xtrackers fund. Published total estimates already account for fund expenses; do not add the underlying fee again. They reduce the investment’s value, rather than arriving as a separate platform bill.

**Tax on dividends.** Dividends are cash paid by companies to their shareholders. For ordinary US share dividends, an eligible Irish fund generally faces 15% US withholding tax. A Singapore-resident individual who is not a US person generally faces 30% on ordinary distributions from a US equity ETF. These percentages apply to dividends, not your whole investment.

**Buying and selling costs.** Your broker charges a trading fee, called commission. SGX also charges 0.0436% with GST on each trade. The gap between buy and sell prices adds a cost too. For funds priced in another currency, you may also pay to convert money when buying and selling.

## One fund or two layers: why it matters

**The four direct Xtrackers listings have no extra Singapore fund fee.** S&P 500, Nasdaq-100, MSCI World and S&P 500 Equal Weight are existing Irish fund share classes listed on SGX. Buying here does not add a second fund. Their stated annual fees range from 0.03% to 0.20%, per DWS factsheets at 30 September 2026.

**The four iFAST funds add a Singapore fund that holds an overseas fund.** They offer Japan, Korea, Taiwan or AI exposure through a local listing. Their published total fee estimates include both layers; the overseas fund fee is not another charge to add.

**Choose the exposure first, then compare ways to buy it.** The direct listings have a simpler structure and lower stated annual fees. But a World or US fund cannot replace a deliberate Japan, Korea, Taiwan or AI allocation. Nasdaq-100 is also more concentrated than a broad world fund.

The iFAST structure may be useful when that specific exposure matters and overseas dealing is unavailable, or local account access is valuable. With overseas access, compare the underlying fund directly against its iFAST version. The extra fund layer is one cost to weigh alongside trading charges and convenience.

## SGX or overseas: what changes?

**Paying in Singapore dollars avoids a separate broker conversion, not all currency costs.** Currency-related dealing costs may arise inside funds or in market-makers’ quoted prices. Exchange-rate movements still affect investment value.

**Direct Xtrackers listings on SGX:** local trading hours and SRS access, subject to your operator. The S&P 500 fund charges 0.03% annually on either exchange. The same share class shares portfolio costs; brokerage and exchange prices can differ.

**iFAST listings on SGX:** local access through another fund, with expenses at both fund levels. The published TER estimates below cover the fund-expense layers.

**Buying overseas:** direct ownership avoids the iFAST fund layer where one exists. It requires overseas market access, suitable trading hours and foreign-currency funding. Broker charges vary. These listings cannot be bought with SRS money.

**What to compare:** fund fees, broker commissions and minimums, account charges, currency terms and live buy/sell prices. Taxes and fund-level trading costs also affect returns. Fees alone cannot identify the better outcome.

## The buy and sell prices matter too

**A low annual fee does not guarantee a cheap purchase.** Delayed Yahoo quotes on 13 October 2026 showed gaps from 0.03% on Nasdaq-100 to 1.46% on equal weight. These were different mid-morning times, from one source, not typical trading costs.

Buying at the higher quoted price costs roughly S$73 more than buying at the midpoint on S$10,000. That is nearly five years of the equal-weight fund’s 0.15% annual fee on a constant holding. The live quote is a separate part of the bill, outside the annual fund fees shown in the calculator.

## What SGX gives you, and what it does not

<div class="sx-access">
<div class="sx-beat"><b>What it gives you</b><ul><li>Paying in Singapore dollars, without converting your cash.</li><li>Access with SRS money, subject to your operator enabling the fund.</li><li>Local trading hours and a local broker, with the option of holding shares in your own Central Depository (CDP) account.</li><li>On iFAST’s funds, a one-share trading lot. The initial offer price is S$1; the market price can change after listing.</li></ul></div>
<div class="sx-beat"><b>What it does not give you</b><ul><li>A lower fee from the Xtrackers fund itself.</li><li>A guarantee that the buy and sell prices will be close together.</li><li>Protection against moves in the currencies of the assets inside.</li><li>CPF access: none of the eight is on CPF Board's list of six ETFs.</li></ul></div>
</div>

**Paying in Singapore dollars does not remove currency risk.** Your investment still owns assets priced in US dollars, yen, won or Taiwan dollars. The currency you pay with does not change what the fund owns.

SRS and CPF also have different rules beyond the investments they permit. [The CPF-versus-SRS comparison](/cpf-vs-srs-top-up/) covers access to the money; [the estate-tax explainer](/us-estate-tax-singapore-investors/) covers what can happen when holdings pass to family.

<details class="src-details">
<summary><b>They arrive after the run</b>: what launch research can and cannot tell us.</summary>

The funds inside the iFAST ETFs had already risen sharply before the launch. In the year to 30 June 2026, Korea returned +187.5% and Taiwan +102.3%, both in US dollars. Japan's Nikkei 225 returned +75.2% in yen; AI & Big Data returned +50.2% in US dollars. These are the prospectus figures, in different currencies, not a like-for-like performance ranking.

Research suggests that theme funds often launch when their subject is attracting attention. A 2023 study in the Review of Financial Studies examined US ETFs from 1993 to 2019. Over their first five years, specialised funds underperformed by about 30% after adjusting for risk.

That finding concerns theme funds such as the AI fund, not whole-country index funds. It is a tendency across hundreds of launches, not a forecast for any one fund. Nothing here predicts where these markets go next.

</details>

## Three questions before buying anything new

1. **What is the biggest company inside, and do I own it already through another fund?** The top ten holdings are on every factsheet, with the date.
2. **Which fund and broker fees are disclosed, and which costs remain variable or unknown?** What is the gap between the buy and sell price today?
3. **Which money can reach it: cash, SRS or CPF?**

The decision comes back to one question: **am I adding different investments, or paying another way to own the same companies?**

<summary><b>Why four funds came from DWS and four from iFAST</b></summary>

DWS listed the existing Irish funds directly on SGX. It registered them with MAS as recognised schemes, with Flow Traders Asia providing buy and sell quotes. Moomoo distributed them during the subscription window.

**iFAST took a different route: a Singapore fund that holds an overseas fund.** Its Singapore company, iFAST Global ETFs Select Series VCC, has four funds, each holding one Xtrackers fund. iFAST charges 0.25% a year for managing them. iFAST Financial and North Point Global provide buy and sell quotes.

The Irish and Luxembourg funds inside are, in the prospectus's words, "not authorised by MAS and will not be directly offered to the public in Singapore". You buy shares in the Singapore fund instead.

**Why use a partner? The history offers a possible explanation, not a confirmed motive.** In August 2020, DWS removed 23 Xtrackers listings from SGX, citing "the current level of the Net Asset Value of the Singapore Shares". These included MSCI Korea 1C and MSCI Taiwan 1C, the versions of the funds that iFAST now holds.

iFAST supplies the Singapore fund and local distribution; DWS manages the underlying fund. The prospectuses establish those roles, but not how the firms divide every commercial cost. iFAST has described issuing ETFs as a "natural extension" of its role, and says the first funds follow its research views. The firms' commercial terms are not public; this explanation of their motives is inference.

**For the investor, the distinction is the fee layers.** The direct DWS listings have one fund's fees. The iFAST listings have the Singapore fund's expenses and the overseas fund's expenses. Neither structure is a US fund, so both sit outside the US estate-tax issue described in the earlier article.

<summary><b>Method</b>: how each figure was computed.</summary>

- **Five-company share** = the sum of the fund's published weights in Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet (both share classes added), from each fund's top-ten holdings at 30 September 2026. All five are in the top ten of each of the four US-heavy funds, so the figure is exact for those funds. For the equal-weight fund, which holds about 500 companies at roughly 0.2% each, the figure is five times 0.2%, approximate.
- **Biggest company** and **ten biggest together** are the issuer's published top-ten figures at the same date, summed.
- **iFAST costs:** the tool and reproduction script use FSM Global’s published TER figures: Japan 0.43% and AI 0.63% (data at 30 September 2026), Korea 0.67% and Taiwan 0.77% (data at 29 September 2026). These pre-launch figures are treated as single-source estimates, not audited actual charges or contractual caps. They are entered as totals; the underlying fund fee is not added again. The source does not provide a component reconciliation, so no rebate or subsidy is assumed.
- **Prospectus components:** Korea lists 0.25% current Singapore management, other Singapore expenses, and an underlying all-in fee of up to 0.45%. Taiwan lists the same Singapore management fee and an underlying all-in fee of up to 0.65%. Korea’s underlying up-to-0.45% includes up-to-0.25% management and a 0.20% fixed fee. That fixed fee belongs to the underlying fund. The 0.90% Singapore management maximum is not an overall TER cap; we found no overall cap in the prospectus.
- **Japan and AI components:** the prospectus lists current Singapore management of 0.25%, other Singapore expenses, and underlying all-in fees of up to 0.09% for Japan and up to 0.35% for AI. FSM Global explicitly calls Japan’s TER an estimate; its AI table says “Total Expense Ratio”. As both funds are pre-launch, we treat both figures as estimates, not historical audited ratios. Neither source supplies a component reconciliation.
- **Day-one gap** = (buy − sell) ÷ midpoint. S$73 is approximately half the 1.46% gap on S$10,000, measured against the quote midpoint, not a separate fee or a fair-value estimate.
- **Menu counts**: SGX's categories (Singapore 7 + 7, China 13 + 2, regional and global 16 + 8 = 53), plus eight.
