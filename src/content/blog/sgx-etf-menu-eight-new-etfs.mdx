**Fewer than eight. Four of the new funds hold the same five American companies, at between a fifth and a third of each fund. What is new is the access: trading in Singapore dollars, SRS money, and the first Korea and Taiwan index funds on SGX since 2020. What is not new is most of what is inside, and the fund itself costs the same whichever exchange you buy it on. The difference is in the dealing, the currency and the wrapper.**

**The timetable first.** Four index funds from DWS's Xtrackers range began trading on SGX on Tuesday 13 October. They track the S&P 500, the Nasdaq-100, MSCI World and the S&P 500 Equal Weight. Four more follow on Thursday 22 October, issued by iFAST and each holding one Xtrackers fund: Japan's Nikkei 225, MSCI Korea, MSCI Taiwan, and an artificial-intelligence theme. [The earlier piece](/sgx-irish-etfs-us-estate-tax/) covered what the first four mean for US estate tax. This one is about the menu and the bill.

## The menu, before and after

SGX counted 53 ETFs and leveraged products at the end of June 2026, holding S$20.5 billion, of which retail investors held 42%. Add this month's eight and the list is about 60 lines. Fifteen of the 53 are China-focused and 14 are Singapore-focused. By building block:

| What you want to own | On SGX at June 2026 | Added in October 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| The developed world in one fund | None; the global funds were US-only | MSCI World (XWR) |
| The United States | SPDR S&P 500 (S27, US-registered, in US$) and a Dow Jones fund | S&P 500 (XUS), S&P 500 Equal Weight (EUS), Nasdaq-100 (XND), in S$ |
| Japan, Korea or Taiwan on its own | One actively managed Japan fund | Nikkei 225 (JPY), MSCI Korea (KOR), MSCI Taiwan (TWD) |
| China | 13 share funds and 2 bond funds | None |
| Asia and emerging markets | 5 regional funds; India, Indonesia and Vietnam on their own | None |
| Singapore shares, bonds, REITs and gold | 14 lines | None |
| A theme | A China electric-vehicle fund, a South-east Asia technology fund | AI & Big Data (XBG) |

<p class="sx-quiet">Counts from SGX's half-year report (June 2026) and the two issuers' prospectuses. The categories are SGX's. Order is by building block, not a ranking.</p>

In plain English: the menu grew where it was thinnest. The developed world is now one line, and Japan, Korea and Taiwan stand on their own. Where the menu was already longest, it did not grow at all.

## What is inside

Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet are the five largest holdings of the S&P 500 fund. Here is how much of each new fund those five companies are, and the single biggest company in each:

|---|---|---|---|
| Nasdaq-100 (XND) | 31.7% | Nvidia 8.4% | 47.0% |
| S&P 500 (XUS) | 30.6% | Nvidia 8.3% | 38.8% |
| AI & Big Data (XBG) | 23.9% | Microsoft 5.8% | 45.4% |
| MSCI World (XWR) | 21.7% | Nvidia 5.8% | 27.6% |
| S&P 500 Equal Weight (EUS) | about 1% | Moderna 0.3% | 2.5% |
| Nikkei 225 (JPY) | 0% | Advantest 12.3% | 49.1% |

<p class="sx-quiet">DWS factsheets, holdings at 30 September 2026. The equal-weight figure is approximate: each of its 500 companies is about 0.2%. Ordered by the first column, not by merit.</p>

In plain English: buying the S&P 500 fund, the Nasdaq-100 fund and the AI fund is not three decisions about three different things. It is one decision about the same companies, made three times, at three different fees. That is not a flaw in the funds. It is what the indexes are, and the equal-weight fund exists precisely to switch it off.

The country funds concentrate the other way: one company inside each. A Taiwan fund is largely a TSMC fund, and still less TSMC than Taiwan. TSMC is 54.0% of the full MSCI Taiwan index (MSCI, September 2026). European fund rules cap any one company at 20% of a fund, or 35% in exceptional market conditions, so the fund tracks a capped version called MSCI Taiwan 20/35. The Nikkei 225 weights companies by share price rather than size, which is how a chip-testing firm, Advantest, comes to be its largest holding.

## What it costs: the four layers

**The fund's own fee** is the one in the adverts, and it is the same wherever you buy the fund: 0.03% a year for the S&P 500 line in London or on SGX. **Dividend tax** is paid inside the fund and differs only by where the fund is registered: an Irish fund loses 15% of its US dividends, a US-registered fund's distributions to a Singapore holder lose 30%. **Dealing** is what changes with the exchange: your broker's commission, SGX's clearing and access fees (0.0436% with GST, charged on every SGX trade and on no overseas trade), the gap between the buying and selling price, and currency conversion each way on anything not in Singapore dollars. **The wrapper or platform** is the fourth layer: iFAST's four lines charge 0.25% a year plus running costs for holding the Xtrackers fund on your behalf, and a unit trust platform charges 0.35% to 0.60% a year on top of the fund.

## Four routes to the S&P 500, on one basis

Bought once, held, sold once. Commission 0.08% with a S$10 minimum on both exchanges. Currency conversion 0.50% each way at a bank-linked broker, or 0.002% with a US$2 minimum at a specialist broker. Spreads 0.10% on the new lines, 0.20% on S27, 0.02% on VOO. Dividend yield 1.04%.

| Route | S$10,000, ten years | S$100,000, ten years | SRS | US estate tax |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| XUS on SGX, in S$ | S$225 | S$2,207 | Yes | No |
| Same fund in London, in US$ | S$316, or S$221 with specialist conversion | S$3,120, or S$2,125 | No | No |
| S27 on SGX, in US$ (US fund) | S$527, or S$432 | S$5,229, or S$4,234 | Yes | Yes |
| VOO in New York, in US$ (US fund) | S$455, or S$360 | S$4,510, or S$3,515 | No | Yes |
| Index unit trust on a platform | S$606 | S$6,060 | Depends on platform | No |

<p class="sx-quiet">Estimates under the stated assumptions, not measured performance. Fund fees per DWS, SSGA and Vanguard; the unit-trust row assumes a 0.10% index fund and a 0.35% platform fee, no sales charge and no dealing costs. The US estate column applies above US$60,000 of US-situated assets held at death; on S$100,000 of S27 it would be about S$6,071. Every figure is reproduced by the script linked below.</p>

Three things stand out. On the Irish fund, SGX and London are within S$100 of each other over ten years if your overseas broker converts currency at specialist rates; the SGX line is about S$900 cheaper on S$100,000 if the conversion runs through a bank-linked broker. The US-registered funds cost two to three times as much, almost all of it dividend tax, before any estate tax. And the index unit trust is the dearest route by a distance, because the platform fee runs every year on the whole holding.

In plain English: the fee you see is the smallest of the four layers. Dividend tax, currency conversion and the platform are where the money goes.

## The iFAST lines: wrapper against conversion

The four feeders cost 0.25% a year plus running costs more than the Xtrackers funds inside them, about S$35 a year on S$10,000. What that buys is a Singapore-dollar line that SRS money can hold, and no currency conversion. At bank conversion rates of 0.50% each way, the overseas route's extra one-off cost is about S$91 on S$10,000, so it becomes the cheaper route after about 2.6 years. At specialist rates the overseas route is cheaper from day one. The reverse holds for SRS money, which cannot reach London or Frankfurt at all.

The two US lines were quoted as tightly as established funds by mid-morning. The World and equal-weight lines were not. A 1.46% gap costs S$73 on a S$10,000 purchase at the quoted price, more than two years of the fund's fee.

## What SGX gives you, and what it does not

<div class="sx-beat"><b>It gives you</b> settlement in Singapore dollars with no conversion. It gives you access with SRS money, which no overseas line offers. It gives you dealing in local hours through a local broker, with the option of holding the shares in your own CDP account. On the iFAST lines it gives you a board lot of one share at an offer price of S$1.</div>

<div class="sx-beat"><b>It does not give you</b> a cheaper fund. The fund fee is the same on every exchange. It does not give you a deeper market. The overseas lines have traded for years and the SGX lines started this month. It does not give you protection from currency. The funds still own dollar, yen, won and Taiwan-dollar assets. It does not give you CPF access. None of the eight is on CPF Board's list of six ETFs.</div>

## They arrive after the run

The iFAST prospectus gives the underlying funds' one-year returns to 30 June 2026. Korea made +187.5% and Taiwan +102.3%, both in US dollars. The Nikkei 225 made +75.2% in yen and the AI fund +50.2% in US dollars. A 2023 study in the Review of Financial Studies looked at US exchange-traded funds from 1993 to 2019. It found that specialised funds, the ones built around a theme, were launched into attention, and over their first five years lost about 30% against the market after adjusting for risk. That describes theme funds such as the AI line, not whole-country index funds, and it is a tendency across hundreds of launches, not a forecast for any one of them. Nothing here is a view on where any of these markets goes next.

## Three questions before buying anything new

1. **What is the biggest company inside, and do I own it already through another fund?** The top ten holdings are on every factsheet, with the date.
2. **What does it cost a year on S$10,000, wrapper and platform included, and what is the gap between the buy and sell price today?**
3. **Which money can reach it: cash, SRS or CPF?**

The menu is longer. The number of different things to own grew by less than eight. One developed-world line, three countries, one theme. The rest is a second helping of companies most index investors already own, now reachable in Singapore dollars and with SRS money. Whether a second helping is what your portfolio lacks is the question only you can answer.

<details class="src-details">
<summary><b>Why four funds came from DWS and four from iFAST</b></summary>

Two routes bring a foreign fund to SGX. DWS took the first for its four largest lines: it registered the Irish funds with MAS as recognised schemes and listed their existing share classes on SGX, with Flow Traders Asia as market maker and moomoo as distributor for the subscription window. The cost and the risk of a thin line fall on DWS.

The second route wraps the foreign fund in a Singapore fund. iFAST set up a Singapore variable capital company, iFAST Global ETFs Select Series VCC, whose four sub-funds each hold one Xtrackers fund, and charges 0.25% a year for doing so. iFAST Financial is the manager and one of the two market makers, with North Point Global. The Luxembourg and Irish funds underneath are, in the prospectus's words, "not authorised by MAS and will not be directly offered to the public in Singapore".

Why would DWS let a partner carry its single-country lines? The record suggests an answer. In August 2020 DWS delisted 23 Xtrackers share classes from SGX, citing "the current level of the Net Asset Value of the Singapore Shares". Xtrackers MSCI Korea 1C and Xtrackers MSCI Taiwan 1C, the share classes the iFAST feeders now hold, were on that list. A feeder moves the listing cost, the marketing and the risk of thin trading onto iFAST, which has a retail platform to sell through, and gives DWS assets in its own fund either way. iFAST's stated reason is that issuing ETFs is a "natural extension" of its role in global investing, and that the first suite follows its research views. That is the observable structure; the commercial terms between the two firms are not public, so the motive is inference, labelled as such.

For the holder the difference is practical: the DWS lines carry one fee, the iFAST lines carry two, and both sit outside US estate tax because neither is a US fund.

<details class="src-details">
<summary><b>Method</b>: how each figure was computed.</summary>

- **Five-company share** = the sum of the fund's published weights in Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet (both share classes added), from each fund's top-ten holdings at 30 September 2026. All five are in the top ten of each of the four US-heavy funds, so the figure is exact for those funds. For the equal-weight fund, which holds about 500 companies at roughly 0.2% each, the figure is five times 0.2%, approximate.
- **Biggest company** and **ten biggest together** are the issuer's published top-ten figures at the same date, summed.
- **Route costs** = running cost + one-off costs. Running = (fund fee + dividend tax) × holding × years, where dividend tax is 15% × yield for the Irish fund and 30% × (yield − fee) for the US funds, which distribute net of fees. One-off, paid to buy and again to sell = max(commission × holding, S$10) + SGX clearing and access fees of 0.0436% on SGX routes + half the spread × holding + currency conversion on non-S$ routes (0.50% × holding, or max(0.002% × holding, S$2.56) at specialist rates). Unit trust = (0.10% + 0.35% + 15% × yield) × holding × years.
- **Wrapper against conversion** = (two conversions at 0.50% less two SGX fee charges) ÷ the wrapper's 0.35% a year: S$91 ÷ S$35 = 2.6 years on S$10,000.
- **Day-one gap** = (buy − sell) ÷ midpoint. S$73 = 1.46% ÷ 2 × S$10,000.
- **Menu counts**: SGX's categories (Singapore 7 + 7, China 13 + 2, regional and global 16 + 8 = 53), plus eight.
- Every computed figure is reproduced by [the script behind this piece](/data/sgx-etf-menu.py).
