**Fewer than eight new things. Four funds hold the same five American companies. If you already own a broad US fund, another label may buy you more of what you have.**

**The same fund charges the same fee on SGX or overseas.** But your total bill can change with currency conversion, buying and selling costs, and any extra fund or platform fee.

**The timetable.** Four Xtrackers funds began trading on Tuesday 13 October 2026: S&P 500, Nasdaq-100, MSCI World and S&P 500 Equal Weight. Four iFAST funds follow on Thursday 22 October: Nikkei 225, MSCI Korea, MSCI Taiwan, and AI & Big Data. Each iFAST fund holds one Xtrackers fund.

[The earlier piece](/sgx-irish-etfs-us-estate-tax/) covers the US estate-tax mechanics. Here, the questions are simpler: what would you own, and what would it cost? SRS means the Supplementary Retirement Scheme, whose savings can buy these SGX-listed ETFs.

## What can you buy that was missing?

**The additions fill gaps in the choice of markets.** SGX gains a fund tracking shares across developed countries, plus separate Japan, Korea and Taiwan index funds. The new US funds trade in Singapore dollars. The China, regional and Singapore ranges do not grow this round.

The June 2026 SGX report counted 53 ETFs and leveraged products, including 15 focused on China and 14 on Singapore. Adding this month's listings puts the menu at about 60, subject to any other listings or removals since June. A longer list is not the same as more variety.

## Different funds can own the same shares

**Different fund names can lead back to the same companies.** Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet are the S&P 500 fund's five largest holdings. They also make up a large part of the Nasdaq-100, AI and developed-world funds.

<SgxOverlap />

**In plain English: more funds can mean more of the same shares.** These funds also own other companies; the overlap does not make them identical. Equal weight reduces the biggest companies' influence by giving each company roughly the same share of the fund.

A country fund can also depend heavily on one company: TSMC is 30.2% of the Taiwan fund, while Samsung Electronics is 32.0% of the Korea fund including its non-voting preference shares. Those percentages are from DWS holdings at 30 September 2026. A country label does not mean your money is spread evenly across its companies.

## Where does the money go?

**Fees taken inside the fund.** The Xtrackers S&P 500 fund charges 0.03% a year whether bought in London or on SGX. iFAST ETFs have another layer: 0.25% a year plus running costs to hold a Xtrackers fund. Our illustrative ETF totals include both layers. They reduce the investment’s value, rather than arriving as a separate platform bill.

**Tax on dividends.** Dividends are cash paid by companies to their shareholders. An Irish fund loses 15% of its US dividends to tax. A US-registered fund’s dividend payments to a Singapore holder lose 30%. These percentages apply to dividends, not your whole investment.

**Buying and selling costs.** Your broker charges a trading fee, called commission. SGX also charges 0.0436% with GST on each trade. The gap between buy and sell prices adds a cost too. For funds priced in another currency, you may also pay to convert money when buying and selling.

**A separate platform fee, for unit trusts.** A unit trust is another type of investment fund. The platforms cited here charge 0.35%–0.60% a year on top of its own fund fee, depending on the account and service. This is separate from the iFAST ETF expenses above.

<SgxFeeLayers />

**In plain English: the advertised fee is only part of the bill.** Some costs come with trading; others repeat every year.

## The iFAST funds: an extra fee for local access

**The Singapore running expenses are not yet known.** Our example assumes 0.10% a year in total. The prospectus does not confirm that amount: several individual expenses may each reach or exceed 0.10%. Adding the 0.25% management fee gives an illustrative S$35 a year on S$10,000.

At bank conversion rates of 0.50% each way, buying overseas adds about S$91 upfront on S$10,000. Under our expense assumption, annual savings recover that extra cost after about 2.6 years. Different expenses change that timing. At specialist conversion rates, overseas is cheaper from day one in this example. SRS cannot buy these funds overseas.

**The tool separates what you own from what you pay.** “What you own” shows repeated companies across any mix you enter. “What it costs” compares ways to buy the same market using your amount and trading costs. The starting mix is an illustration, not a suggested portfolio.

## The buy and sell prices matter too

**A low annual fee does not guarantee a cheap purchase.** Delayed Yahoo quotes on 13 October 2026 showed gaps from 0.03% on Nasdaq-100 to 1.46% on equal weight. These were different mid-morning times, from one source, not typical trading costs.

The wider gap implies S$73 on a S$10,000 purchase at the quoted price, more than two years of that fund's fee. The table above assumes narrower gaps; the live quote is a separate part of the bill.

## What SGX gives you, and what it does not

<div class="sx-access">
<div class="sx-beat"><b>What it gives you</b><ul><li>Paying in Singapore dollars, without converting your cash.</li><li>Access with SRS money, subject to your operator enabling the fund.</li><li>Local trading hours and a local broker, with the option of holding shares in your own Central Depository (CDP) account.</li><li>On iFAST's funds, one-share purchases at the S$1 offer price.</li></ul></div>
<div class="sx-beat"><b>What it does not give you</b><ul><li>A lower fee from the Xtrackers fund itself.</li><li>A guarantee that the buy and sell prices will be close together.</li><li>Protection against moves in the currencies of the assets inside.</li><li>CPF access: none of the eight is on CPF Board's list of six ETFs.</li></ul></div>
</div>

**Paying in Singapore dollars does not remove currency risk.** Your investment still owns assets priced in US dollars, yen, won or Taiwan dollars. The currency you pay with does not change what the fund owns.

SRS and CPF also have different rules beyond the investments they permit. [The CPF-versus-SRS comparison](/cpf-vs-srs-top-up/) covers access to the money; [the estate-tax explainer](/us-estate-tax-singapore-investors/) covers what can happen when holdings pass to family.

<details class="src-details">
<summary><b>They arrive after the run</b>: what launch research can and cannot tell us.</summary>

The funds inside the iFAST ETFs had already risen sharply before the launch. In the year to 30 June 2026, Korea returned +187.5% and Taiwan +102.3%, both in US dollars. Japan's Nikkei 225 returned +75.2% in yen; AI & Big Data returned +50.2% in US dollars. These are the prospectus figures, in different currencies, not a like-for-like performance ranking.

Research suggests that theme funds often launch when their subject is attracting attention. A 2023 study in the Review of Financial Studies examined US ETFs from 1993 to 2019. Over their first five years, specialised funds lost about 30% against the market after accounting for risk.

That finding concerns theme funds such as the AI fund, not whole-country index funds. It is a tendency across hundreds of launches, not a forecast for any one fund. Nothing here predicts where these markets go next.

</details>

## Three questions before buying anything new

1. **What is the biggest company inside, and do I own it already through another fund?** The top ten holdings are on every factsheet, with the date.
2. **What would S$10,000 cost to hold each year, including every fund fee and any platform charge?** What is the gap between the buy and sell price today?
3. **Which money can reach it: cash, SRS or CPF?**

More funds are now within reach of local cash and SRS money. The question is still yours: **am I adding different investments, or paying another way to own the same companies?**

<details class="src-details">
<summary><b>Why four funds came from DWS and four from iFAST</b></summary>

DWS listed the existing Irish funds directly on SGX. It registered them with MAS as recognised schemes, with Flow Traders Asia providing buy and sell quotes. Moomoo distributed them during the subscription window. DWS carries the listing cost and the risk that few people trade them.

**iFAST took a different route: a Singapore fund that holds an overseas fund.** Its Singapore company, iFAST Global ETFs Select Series VCC, has four funds, each holding one Xtrackers fund. iFAST charges 0.25% a year for managing them. iFAST Financial and North Point Global provide buy and sell quotes.

The Irish and Luxembourg funds inside are, in the prospectus's words, "not authorised by MAS and will not be directly offered to the public in Singapore". You buy shares in the Singapore fund instead.

**Why use a partner? The history offers a possible explanation, not a confirmed motive.** In August 2020, DWS removed 23 Xtrackers listings from SGX, citing "the current level of the Net Asset Value of the Singapore Shares". These included MSCI Korea 1C and MSCI Taiwan 1C, the versions of the funds that iFAST now holds.

Using iFAST moves the local listing cost, marketing and risk of little trading onto a partner with a retail platform. DWS still receives money into its overseas funds. iFAST has described issuing ETFs as a "natural extension" of its role, and says the first funds follow its research views. The firms' commercial terms are not public; this explanation of their motives is inference.

**For the investor, the distinction is the fee layers.** The direct DWS listings have one fund's fees. The iFAST listings have the Singapore fund's expenses and the overseas fund's expenses. Neither structure is a US fund, so both sit outside the US estate-tax issue described in the earlier article.

</details>

<details class="src-details">
<summary><b>Method</b>: how each figure was computed.</summary>

- **Five-company share** = the sum of the fund's published weights in Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet (both share classes added), from each fund's top-ten holdings at 30 September 2026. All five are in the top ten of each of the four US-heavy funds, so the figure is exact for those funds. For the equal-weight fund, which holds about 500 companies at roughly 0.2% each, the figure is five times 0.2%, approximate.
- **Biggest company** and **ten biggest together** are the issuer's published top-ten figures at the same date, summed.
- **Route costs** = running cost + one-off costs. Running = (fund fee + dividend tax) × holding × years, where dividend tax is 15% × yield for the Irish fund and 30% × (yield − fee) for the US funds, which distribute net of fees. One-off, paid to buy and again to sell = max(commission × holding, S$10) + SGX clearing and access fees of 0.0436% on SGX routes + half the spread × holding + currency conversion on non-S$ routes (0.50% × holding, or max(0.002% × holding, S$2.56) at specialist rates). Unit trust = (0.10% + 0.35% + 15% × yield) × holding × years.
- **iFAST expense assumption:** 0.10% a year for the combined Singapore running expenses is an illustrative input, not a prospectus total or cap. The prospectus says individual expenses may each reach or exceed 0.10%. The tool and script therefore show scenarios, not confirmed expense ratios. For Korea, 0.80% assumes 0.25% Singapore management + 0.10% Singapore expenses + 0.45% underlying fund expenses. The underlying fund’s up-to-0.45% already includes its up-to-0.25% management fee and 0.20% fixed fee (prospectus, Appendix IV, sections 6C–6D). The fixed fee does not belong to the Singapore layer.
- **Wrapper against conversion, under that assumption** = (two conversions at 0.50% less two SGX fee charges) ÷ the wrapper's 0.35% a year: S$91 ÷ S$35 = 2.6 years on S$10,000.
- **Day-one gap** = (buy − sell) ÷ midpoint. S$73 = 1.46% ÷ 2 × S$10,000.
- **Menu counts**: SGX's categories (Singapore 7 + 7, China 13 + 2, regional and global 16 + 8 = 53), plus eight.
- Every computed figure is reproduced by [the script behind this piece](/data/sgx-etf-menu.py).

</details>
