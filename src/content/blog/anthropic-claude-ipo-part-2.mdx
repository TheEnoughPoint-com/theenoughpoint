In August, I wrote about an IPO I was excited to follow. Now that Anthropic has publicly filed its registration statement, I have a more useful question: which parts of my enthusiasm survive closer scrutiny?

My answer is mixed. The demand story looks stronger. My confidence that an understandable product makes an understandable investment needs more work.

This is a follow-up to [Anthropic Claude IPO: What RE Thinks, published on 19 August](/anthropic-claude-ipo-what-re-thinks/). I am keeping that original view visible rather than rewriting it with hindsight. The tables below separate supported calls, corrections and questions that remain open.

## What changed on 26 October

Anthropic [announced its public Form S-1 on 26 October 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/s1-sec). It proposes a Nasdaq listing under **ANT**. The offering price range and number of shares have not been determined.

That replaces my deliberately temporary ANTH ticker. It does not settle my earlier valuation scenarios or establish that trading has begun.

For this comparison, all amounts are in US dollars. H1 means January to June; Q2 means April to June. GAAP refers to the standard accounting basis, rather than a company's adjusted earnings measure.

## The key warnings

<img src="/img/Published/anthropic-claude-ipo-part-2-warnings.jpg" alt="Key warnings illustrated: fixed infrastructure commitments, research costs outside gross margin, limited shareholder control, and AI safety risks. The text below explains each warning." width="1200" height="896" loading="lazy" className="w-full h-auto rounded-lg my-6" />

### 1. Spending commitments can outlast customer demand

The financing question deserves as much attention as the next model release. Long-term capacity commitments can remain payable when customers cut usage. I would not add overlapping contract disclosures into a single headline total without reconciliation. [Commitment analysis](https://quantdata.uk/research/anthropic-s1-ipo-analysis)

My concern is the timing. Capacity has to be available when customers need it. Paying for capacity before the related sales arrive can strain cash even when demand is growing.

That is why I would separate present operating performance from future funding needs. Success at one does not automatically solve the other.

### 2. A strong gross margin leaves substantial work unpaid for

The supplemental table implies an **85.4% H1 gross margin**, but research and development cost **$9.741bn** outside that calculation. The classification matters. [Expense analysis](https://research.aardvarklabs.co/research/ant/earnings/2026-s1/ant-2026-s1-analysis)

I read gross margin as one stage of the calculation. Research, distribution and other operating expenses still need funding before I can discuss the whole business's earnings.

An impressive percentage is less useful if I do not ask which costs sit outside it. I made a related mistake in August by putting different measures of profitability beside each other.

### 3. Owning shares will not mean controlling the business

A founder-held share carries **50.1% of general voting power**, while a separate trust elects a board majority. Public investors therefore face limits on their influence. [Governance review](https://ppc.land/anthropic-files-for-nasdaq-ipo-with-16-3bn-in-first-half-revenue/)

I do not treat that structure as automatically good or bad. I treat it as part of what is being purchased. Confidence in a mission does not remove the need to understand who makes decisions when interests diverge.

### 4. Safety belongs in the business assessment

The risk disclosures include harmful autonomous behaviour and oversight failures. [Risk coverage](https://www.axios.com/2026/10/26/anthropic-ipo-risk-factors-humanity)

My interpretation is that these concerns can affect liability, deployment and customer confidence. I would not dismiss them simply because they are harder to put into a valuation spreadsheet.

Nor would I rank them confidently against every other risk. The correction to my August view is to widen the assessment, not replace one sweeping claim with another.

## The key reliefs

### 1. The headline loss is not equivalent cash burn

The **$80.748bn H1 net loss** includes **$72.388bn of non-cash financing revaluations**. Those entries do not represent equal cash payments in that period. [Loss reconciliation](https://ppc.land/anthropic-files-for-nasdaq-ipo-with-16-3bn-in-first-half-revenue/)

That distinction matters before I react to the headline. It does not make the financing arrangements harmless: conversion terms and dilution still affect shareholders. I would avoid both extremes—calling the full loss cash burn, or dismissing everything non-cash as irrelevant.

### 2. Operating cash flow was positive

H1 operating cash flow was **$3.929bn positive**. Payment timing and future investment needs mean this alone does not establish self-funded growth. [Cash-flow tables](https://quantdata.uk/research/anthropic-s1-ipo-analysis)

For me, that is reassuring evidence to put beside the earnings figures. I would still want to see how repeatable it is. A single reporting period cannot answer every question about financing a rapidly expanding business.

### 3. Demand extends beyond my own experience

More than **11,900 customers** generated at least **$100,000 each in trailing-year revenue** by September. That provides a broader commercial signal than my personal use of Claude. [Customer disclosures](https://ppc.land/anthropic-files-for-nasdaq-ipo-with-16-3bn-in-first-half-revenue/)

I find that distinction healthy. My experience explains why I became interested; it cannot establish the size or durability of the market. I need evidence that remains useful even if my own experience is unusually positive.

## What I would watch next

I would keep the next review focused on three questions: the final offer terms, the cost of future capacity, and whether operating progress persists across comparable periods.

I am not carrying forward an automatic purchase decision from a valuation scenario written before the public filing. The actual price, share count and resulting dilution need to be assessed together.

I would also keep business progress separate from share-price behaviour. [Our leveraged-ETF article](/leveraged-etf-daily-reset/) explores a different product, but makes a relevant distinction: being right about a company does not settle the outcome of a particular investment.

The first-day rally and shakeout I imagined in August remain predictions. They do not belong in the correct column just because a public filing now exists.

## RE’s conclusion

My August argument was directionally stronger on demand and the arrival of quarterly operating profit. It was too comfortable about the leap from using a useful product to understanding its investment risks.

I still find the company worth studying. I now want a better account of the capital required to sustain its growth, alongside the eventual price of participating in it.

At TheEnoughPoint, I want revisiting an opinion to be part of the process. If I publish a view before all the information is available, I should also show what changes when more arrives.

The question for me is no longer simply whether Claude can become important. It is whether the business can turn that importance into durable earnings on terms that make sense for its shareholders.
